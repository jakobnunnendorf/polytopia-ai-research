\section{Question and hypothesis}
\textbf{The idea (David, 28 Nov 2026, issue \#166).} Experiment 29 for research: the research factor (move type 0) becomes a lasting
level in the state, which the policy moves one step per turn. In 29 the economy level passed against r15 ($+195$),
but the gain came from lowering economy overall, not from its timing. This experiment asks the same question for
research, with economy at r15's weights.

\textbf{Prediction} (before any run of this experiment):
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \emph{r15's level is about right when fixed.} In the action check, $\times2$ every turn is within $\pm100$ of
        r15 (spend3's research action: $-36$), and $\times0.5$ loses at least 200 points (probe-perf, with r15's research
        factor $\times0.67$, is about 340 points behind r15).
  \item \emph{More research late.} A tech scores at once and needs no payback, unlike an economy build: without
        income in the state, the path is above $\times1$ in at least 5 of the last 10 turns.
  \item \emph{A small gain.} The chosen policy's mean difference against r15 is between 0 and $+150$, and it does
        \emph{not} pass the \#171 rule.
  \item \emph{The model is optimistic, as in 29:} its predicted gain exceeds the measured gain by more than 200
        points.
  \item \emph{Income adds little}: within $\pm 60$ of the version without, not significant.
\end{enumerate}
\textbf{Against the prediction:} a PASS would mean that the timing of research pays, since r15's fixed research
level was already tuned (experiment 17's sweep).

\section{Mechanism}
As experiment 29, with the action set \texttt{research5} (\texttt{rl\_value::strategic}): the base bot's research factor (move type 0)
times 0.5, 0.75, 1, 1.33 or 2. The levels are symmetric around $\times1$ because the direction is not known in
advance (29's economy levels went down to $\times0.25$ because the check had shown that direction). At $\times1$ the
weights are r15's, bit for bit (a test checks). The level starts at $\times1$ and moves at most one step per learner
turn; a state without a policy entry keeps the level.

\textbf{State}: (turn, level before the turn) and (turn, level, income / 3). \textbf{Value iteration}: \texttt{rl-value
fit-level}, certainty-equivalence model, $\gamma = 1$, one backward sweep, min-count $m$ (see 29). \textbf{Data}: a
random walk over the levels from $\times1$ (\texttt{--driver walk}).

\section{Interpretation}
\textbf{Prediction 1 ($\times2$ within $\pm100$, $\times0.5$ at least 200 worse): right} ($-36$ and $-423$).
\textbf{Prediction 2 (above $\times1$ in at least 5 of the last 10 turns): right} (all 10 at $\times2$).
\textbf{Prediction 3 (0 to $+150$, no PASS): wrong.} Both versions pass, $+152$ and $+158$.
\textbf{Prediction 4 (model optimistic by more than 200): right} ($+646$ predicted, $+152$ measured).
\textbf{Prediction 5 (income adds little): right} ($+6$, $p = 1.0$).

\textbf{Here the timing pays, unlike economy in 29.} The learned path beats both fixed levels it is made of:
$\times1.33$ every turn by $+54$ and $\times2$ every turn by $+89$, both PASS. In 29, the economy policy was no better
than a static cut. The check shows why a fixed level cannot do it: $\times2$ every turn wins the most techs but costs
expansion early (2.32 cities against 2.62, income 15.3 against 17.1), and so ends behind $\times1.33$. The policy
researches moderately in the opening, while cities and income compound, and at full $\times2$ from turn 14, when a
tech scores at once and there is no time left for an expansion to pay back. That is the mechanism of prediction 2,
and it is worth more than prediction 3 allowed.

\textbf{The model ranks well and values badly, as in 29.} ``Keep $\times1$'' is valued at 6{,}488, about 700 below
r15's real score, so the predicted gain ($+646$) is four times the measured one. The opening zigzag between
$\times1.33$ and $\times2$ is a second sign of near-equal values there; the late part of the path is the stable one.

\section{Threats to validity}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{The fixed levels compared are two.} A ramp written by hand (e.g.\ $\times1.33$ to turn 13, $\times2$
        after) might match the policy; then the RL contribution is finding the ramp, not a schedule no simple rule
        gives. The $\times2$ level was chosen after seeing the path, but not on the evaluation seeds.
  \item \emph{Crowded games.} With 15 opponents the policy without income is $-44$ (not significant; information
        only): the late research push may not pay where fights decide more.
  \item \emph{Model bias from the random walk}, as in 29: the model's values cannot be read as expected scores; only
        the gate measures the policy.
\end{itemize}

\section{Verdict and next step}
\textbf{PASS against r15} for both versions ($+152$, $+158$; with income not significantly better, and its p10 is
lower), and the (turn, level) policy also passes against the best fixed level ($+54$) and against $\times2$ every turn
($+89$). Chosen version: (turn, level), $m = 500$. Whether it enters the RL line is for David and Jakob to decide.

Next step (one idea): combine it with 29's economy policy. Economy and research share the same stars (cutting
economy to $\times0.5$ raised techs from 19.3 to 20.4 in 29's check), so the two gains may overlap. First play both
schedules together and gate that against each alone; if the gains overlap, a joint state (turn, economy level,
research level) is the experiment that can learn the interaction.
